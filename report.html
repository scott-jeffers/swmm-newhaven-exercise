1The question

Towns vacuum catch-basin sumps to keep sediment out of rivers and Long Island Sound, but vacuum trucks are expensive. Is it good enough to clean only some basins, or to sweep the streets instead? We modeled six versions of the same storm-drain system. Only the maintenance changes: pipes, rain and land use stay the same.

2How SWMM is made to represent a catch basin

SWMM has no "catch basin sump" object, so we build one from three standard pieces. This is the key modeling trick to understand.

The outlet pipe keeps its real invert. The node floor is dropped 2 ft (CTDOT standard sump), so 2 ft of water sits below the pipe between storms. When sediment fills the sump, the floor rises and less volume is left.

① Buildup on the street

Dirt accumulates between storms (exponential, 0.4/day, up to 80 lb/ac on roads, 40 on lawns and roofs, 60 on the commercial lot).

② Washoff in the storm

Runoff carries it into the catch basins. We set the washoff coefficient so runoff averages about 100 mg/L of TSS, close to the national (NURP) urban median.

③ Settling in the sump

A treatment expression on each storage node removes TSS according to how long water stays there (hydraulic residence time, HRT, in hours):

Cout = Cin · e−k·HRT,  k = 0.5 /hr

Bigger sump → longer HRT → more settling.

[LANDUSES]  ;; street sweeping (S4, S5): every 14 days, 70% of buildup reachable
ROAD       14      0.70     7
[WASHOFF]   ;; ... sweeping efficiency 50%
ROAD       TSS     EXP      0.95  1.5   50   0

LINK_OFFSETS = ELEVATION, so pipe inverts don't change when the node floor is lowered. Lowering the floor is what creates the sump.

3Where the sediment ends up

Every pound washed off ends up in one of three places: removed by the sweeper before it can wash off, trapped in a sump, or carried out the outfall.

Why sweeping helps less than it seems. The sweeper picked up — lb, yet only — lb less reached the outfall (S4 vs S3). Curbs refill within days, most of what is swept would never have washed off anyway, and runoff from yards and roofs is never swept.

5Inside one storm: October 30, 2025

The largest storm in the record, 1.97 in. Sumps cut TSS concentration most at the start and end of the storm, when flows are low and water has time to settle. At the 0.68 in/hr peak, water rushes straight through and the scenarios converge. 6Eighteen months, month by month

TSS reaching the outfall each month. Wet months drive the load. 2025 was a dry year at Tweed, about 32.5 in against a normal of roughly 47 in, so annual loads here are on the low side.

7The answer depends on one number

The settling rate k is a guess (0.5/hr, from the preliminary study). Re-running with other values changes the answer a lot. The ranking Full > Hybrid > None never changes, but the size of the gap is only as good as k. Massachusetts DEP credits deep-sump catch basins with 25% TSS removal (dashed line), which suggests k = 0.5 is optimistic. This is exactly what field monitoring would calibrate.

8Questions for discussion

  1. Hybrid cleaning keeps the downstream basins clean. Using the basin map, which two basins would you clean instead if you could only afford two? Why?
  2. Why do sumps help less during the peak of the Oct 30 storm? What real process (scour, resuspension) might make it even worse?
  3. SWMM never "fills" a sump on its own. The 0.5 ft "full" depth was imposed by hand. How would you use monthly Sludge Judge readings to make this time-varying?
  4. If outfall grab samples average 60 mg/L, which parameter would you adjust first, and in which direction?
  5. The model assumes an uncleaned basin still passes flow freely. What happens to pipe capacity and street flooding if it doesn't? Check the baseline design storms in the viewer.
Model assumptions, limitations and files
  • Network: the user-edited baseline (OF-21708 system: 7 catch basins, 7 pipes, 48 subcatchments). Pipe inverts were estimated from the DEM, so cover is shallow (0.4–1.5 ft).
  • Rain: Tweed-New Haven ASOS (HVN) hourly precipitation via the Iowa Environmental Mesonet, Jan 2025 – Jun 2026 (46.9 in). Snow is treated as rain.
  • Sumps: 4 × 4 ft storage nodes with a 2.0 ft sump when clean and 0.5 ft when 75% full. Sediment depth is fixed for the whole run.
  • Settling: first-order on HRT, k = 0.5/hr. There is no scour. Trapped mass is SWMM's "Mass Reacted"; per-basin values come from 5-minute loads scaled to the system mass balance.
  • Sweeping: curbside ROAD land use only, every 14 days, availability 0.70, efficiency 50%.
  • Infiltration: Curve Number ≈ 68 (NRCS soil group B/D). Monthly evaporation is typical for Connecticut.
